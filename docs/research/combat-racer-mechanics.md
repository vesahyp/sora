# What people love in combat racers

Research notes, 2026-10-03, for Sora. The question was: Super Cars II
on its own was too tame, what makes Death Rally, Mad Max, Burnout and
their kin fun, and which of those mechanics fit a one-thumb phone game.
Sources at the end. Where a claim is mine and not the source's, it says
so.

## The short version

1. **Ramming is the game, guns are the seasoning.** Death Rally's
   machine gun has limited ammo on purpose, so the primary weapon is
   the car itself: ram opponents into walls, mines and each other.
   Burnout 3 made full-contact racing necessary to win. Mad Max is
   side-swipes, grinding and boost-rams first, the harpoon second.
2. **Aggression is rewarded immediately.** Burnout refills the boost
   on a takedown and lengthens the meter. Death Rally pays for a wreck
   on the spot and lets you loot the track. The reward has to arrive in
   the race, not on the result screen.
3. **Boost is earned by driving dangerously.** Burnout: drifting, near
   misses, oncoming lanes. The player's own risk is the fuel. Boost that
   just refills over time teaches nothing.
4. **Leading is dangerous.** Death Rally's players' wisdom: get in front
   early and you cop the flak and get wrecked in 30 seconds; sit behind
   a car and shoot it, but watch for mines. This gives a race a shape
   beyond "be fastest".
5. **Pickups keep you reading the road.** Death Rally: money, turbo,
   ammo, wrenches, and the count on the track stays constant: take one
   and another spawns. Rock n' Roll Racing: money and health on the
   course, mines in the road. Pickups give a reason to take a line that
   is not the fastest.
6. **A wreck is a pause, not an end.** Death Rally: 100% damage is a
   destroyed car, repaired for money. Burnout: a crash costs boost and
   a segment, then you are back. Nobody sits out the race.
7. **The car peels like an onion.** Mad Max's designers' phrase: damage
   is visible and progressive, and a damaged car is a worse car. Death
   Rally shows damage as a colour strip under the name.
8. **Opponents react to you.** Burnout's rivals get hostile when rammed,
   with an arrow showing their mood. Death Rally has a named nemesis.
   An AI that holds a grudge reads as alive.
9. **Short races, many of them.** Death Rally: four cars, 4 to 6 laps,
   prize money scaling with difficulty, points toward a league of 20
   drivers that moves on its own between races.
10. **A market with bad ideas in it.** Death Rally's underground market:
    mines for one race, rocket fuel that hurts you, a bribe to sabotage
    the best car, a loan shark with interest. Choices with a catch.

## Per game

### Death Rally (Remedy, 1996)

- Top-down, four cars a race, three track tiers by difficulty.
- Machine gun with limited ammo; dual guns on the better cars; guns
  disabled for the first two seconds of a race.
- Mines (one race), spiked bumpers for ramming damage (one race),
  rocket fuel (faster turbo that damages you), sabotage (a bribe to
  damage the leader's car), a loan shark.
- Turbo meter, refilled by a lightning pickup; acceleration zones on
  the hard tracks.
- Track pickups: turbo, ammo, small and large money, small and large
  wrench (repair), and a "mushroom" that gives a random effect. The
  number of objects on the track is constant.
- Four armour tiers per car; damage shown as a strip; 100% is wrecked.
  Repair costs scale with the car's tier.
- Ramming damages both cars by armour; spikes shift it to the victim.
- Prize money by place and difficulty, a championship of 20 drivers
  whose points move even in races you are not in.
- Strategy from the players: take every pickup, kill everyone without
  taking damage, do not close in for the kill at once, keep driving.

### Burnout 3: Takedown (Criterion, 2004)

- Boost from drifting, near misses and oncoming lanes; usable at once.
- Takedown: ram a rival until it crashes. Fills the boost and extends
  the meter, up to four times its size.
- Crashing costs boost and a segment; aftertouch lets a crashed car
  take a rival with it.
- Rival hostility scales with how much you rammed them, shown by an
  arrow above the car.

### Mad Max (Avalanche, 2015)

- Ramming, side-swipe (a specific upgrade: a lateral slam for big
  damage), grinding, boost-ramming, all working with the onboard
  weapons.
- The harpoon: tethered, aimed at a vehicle's weak point with slowed
  time; later rips parts off and drags bodies.
- "The car is like an onion you peel pieces off": progressive, visible
  damage.
- Powersliding and high-speed collisions were what reviewers called
  liberating.

### Rock n' Roll Racing (Silicon & Synapse, 1993)

- Isometric, two players, weapons and ramming encouraged.
- Oil slicks, tacks, mines, grenades, plasma, missiles, homing orbs;
  ramming into rails.
- Money and health pickups on the course, mines on the road, jumps.
- A money-for-laps economy and a planet-by-planet career.

### Super Cars II (Magnetic Fields, 1991)

- Top-down, four cars on one screen, nine tracks across three tiers.
- Front and rear missiles, armour, damage repaired for money.
- A shop with a haggling salesman; comic interludes (tax form, police
  quiz) between races.
- Simultaneous two-player on the same screen.

## What Sora took, and why

- Guns that fire themselves (the Räkkä rule): the thumb has one job.
  Death Rally's limited ammo became a gun that overheats plus missiles
  and mines bought by the shot.
- Boost from drifting, ramming and wrecks; a wreck fills the tank
  (Burnout's takedown).
- Ramming damage by closing speed, mass and armour; the victim pays.
- Wrecks burn and respawn half fixed; a bounty on the spot.
- Pickups on the road, respawning, constant count.
- Guns off for three seconds after the lights.
- Hostility per rival (Burnout's arrow) as a grudge per car, raised by
  rams, shots and wrecks; the bot leans on, blocks and aims at whoever
  it holds it against, and everyone goes for the leader.
- Not taken yet: the harpoon, sabotage and loans,
  acceleration zones, a league that moves on its own, same-screen
  two-player.
